01

The Problem

The issue

Kazakhstan has spent the last decade making buildings more accessible: ramps at entrances, accessible toilets, lifts in new housing. Yet a person in a wheelchair, a blind pedestrian or a deaf commuter does not live inside one building. They have to get there. Between the bus stop and the ramp lie sidewalks, kerbs, underpasses, stairs and pedestrian crossings, and this is where the chain of accessibility most often breaks. This project focuses on that gap: the inaccessible journey through public urban space, using Astana as the pilot city.

Ordinary navigation apps make the problem invisible. They optimise for distance and time and assume that every path can be walked by everyone. For a wheelchair user, a route that is 300 metres shorter but contains a flight of stairs is not a shorter route; it is no route at all.

Who is affected, where, and at what scale

According to the Minister of Labour and Social Protection, about 752,000 people with a registered disability lived in Kazakhstan in 2026, or 3.7% of the population; 56% were of working age, 29% of pension age and 15% were children (Bizmedia.kz, 2026). An analysis of official data for 2011–2023 found that the number grew by 28.7% over twelve years, and that the number of children with disabilities more than doubled between 2010 and 2022, from 49,349 to 104,260 (Forbes.kz, 2025). The European Training Foundation notes that 58% of persons with disabilities live in urban areas (ETF, 2026), so city streets are where most of them have to move.

These numbers are the lower bound. The World Health Organization estimates that 1.3 billion people, about 16% of humanity, experience significant disability (WHO, 2022). Older people, parents with prams and people with temporary injuries also depend on step-free, well-marked routes.

How the problem is changing over time

Three trends make the issue more pressing. First, the registered population is growing every year (711,800 in 2022, 725,000 in 2023, 737,000 in 2024; Bizmedia.kz, 2026), and Kazakhstan’s population is ageing. Second, the state has committed to change. Kazakhstan ratified the UN Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities (CRPD) in 2015, adopted a new Social Code in 2023 that recognises equal rights and non-discrimination, and is now implementing a Concept of Inclusive Policy for 2025–2030 (UN CRPD Committee, 2024; Prime Minister of Kazakhstan, 2026). Third, the gap between official progress and lived experience remains wide. The government reports that 67% of about 43,000 facilities on its interactive accessibility map are fully adapted, up from 53% in 2024 (Prime Minister of Kazakhstan, 2026). Independent reporting, by contrast, quotes experts who estimate that around 80% of facilities are inaccessible, and found that only 11 of 3,200 rated facilities on the map were accessible to wheelchair users (CABAR.asia, n.d.).

In 2024 the UN Committee on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities reviewed Kazakhstan for the first time. It criticised the “narrow scope of accessibility requirements, emphasizing physical measures such as ramps” and the lack of legally binding mechanisms to enforce accessibility standards. It also noted the absence of a national strategy on personal mobility and of orientation training for people with visual impairments (UN CRPD Committee, 2024, paras. 19, 43).

The social-science question

The barriers themselves are physical, but their persistence is social and political. Three mechanisms keep the inaccessible journey in place.

  • Fragmented responsibility. A single walk crosses the jurisdiction of the city akimat, road-maintenance contractors, building owners and traffic police. Each actor can comply with its own standard while the route as a whole remains impassable. No institution “owns” the journey.
  • Who counts and who is counted. Accessibility is measured facility by facility, mostly for public buildings, and the people affected are counted through a medical certification system. What is not measured is not managed, and the experiences of disabled pedestrians rarely reach planners (Yespolova, 2020).
  • Voice and power. Disabled people have the most precise knowledge of where routes fail, but the least power over street design. The CRPD principle “nothing about us without us” is written into law but not yet into everyday planning (UN CRPD Committee, 2024).
The social-science question

The research question that follows is therefore: why do accessibility reforms in Kazakhstan improve individual buildings faster than they improve the routes between them, and can better, citizen-generated data shift that balance? The problem is real for the hundreds of thousands of registered people with disabilities, and for everyone the register does not count.

Several institutions produce data on disability and accessibility in Kazakhstan, each answering a different question (Table 1).

What the data shows

Official figures show steady growth in the registered population with disabilities (Figure 1). The number rose from 626,740 in 2013 to about 752,000 in 2026, while its share of the population stayed close to 3.6–3.7% (Forbes.kz, 2025; Bizmedia.kz, 2026). Public spending followed: combined spending from the state budget and the social insurance fund reached about 1 trillion tenge in 2025 and is planned at 1.1 trillion in 2026 (Bizmedia.kz, 2026). None of these figures, however, says whether the streets between home and a service can actually be used.

For accessibility, the government reports rapid progress. The share of fully adapted facilities on the accessibility map rose from 53% in 2024 to 64% in 2025 and 67% in 2026, with a target of 80% (Prime Minister of Kazakhstan, 2026). Earlier, the ministry had reported that 90.7% of social and transport infrastructure facilities were accessible (Ministry of Labour and Social Protection, n.d.). The two series cannot both be right, which shows how differently accessibility is defined.

My own data: the street-level gap

To test what is known about routes rather than buildings, I used the open pedestrian network that powers this project’s route planner. Using OpenStreetMap data for the Nurzhol Boulevard area of Astana (downloaded 23 November 2026), I counted how many path segments carry accessibility information (Figure 2). The area contains 3,762 mapped path segments, 112 crossing segments and 56 stair segments. Only one crossing has any information about an audible signal, one has information about tactile paving, and two have information about the kerb. The surface type is known for only 15% of segments.

In other words, a blind pedestrian asking “does this crossing have a sound signal?” gets an answer for less than 1% of crossings in the most showcased part of the capital. Data exists for stairs, so the planner can avoid them, but is almost absent for features that matter to blind, deaf and low-vision pedestrians.

Where the data is silent, and who falls through the gaps

  • People without certification. The UN Committee found that persons with disabilities are under-represented in national statistics because of the use of the medical model of disability, and recommended the Washington Group questions instead (UN CRPD Committee, 2024, paras. 65–66). The contrast between Kazakhstan’s 3.7% and the WHO’s global estimate of 16% suggests how many people may be missing (WHO, 2022).
  • Journeys, not buildings. No public dataset records barriers on sidewalks and crossings. UNDP notes that accessibility maps mostly contain government buildings and little about private organisations (UNDP Kazakhstan, n.d.).
  • Intersectional groups. The Committee also found little data on women, older people, children and migrants with disabilities (UN CRPD Committee, 2024, para. 65).
  • Quality over time. A ramp can exist and still be unusable. In Astana’s subsidised housing, accessibility features were present but often poorly built or not maintained (Kaiyrly, 2025). A one-time audit cannot capture ice, construction fences or a broken sound signal.

How far can the numbers be trusted?

The registry figures are reliable for what they measure: certified disability, used to pay benefits. They are a poor measure of how many people face barriers on the street. The accessibility map is less reliable: its methodology is not explained in the releases I found, its percentages differ between releases, and activists told CABAR that people with disabilities hardly used it (CABAR.asia, n.d.). OpenStreetMap is transparent and openly licensed, but its accessibility tags depend on volunteers and are almost empty in Astana. The honest conclusion is that nobody currently knows how accessible Astana’s routes are. That data gap is the space this project works in.

03

Current State of Research

Research relevant to accessible urban journeys comes from two worlds that rarely meet: social research on disability in the post-Soviet region, and computer and geographic science on mapping and routing. I organise the literature by approach rather than by author.

Approach 1: Disability as a social and political experience in the post-Soviet region

Scholarship on Eastern Europe and the former Soviet Union shows that disability policy in the region still carries Soviet legacies: a medical and institutional view of disability, welfare built around certification and benefits, and limited participation of disabled people in policy-making (Rasell & Iarskaia-Smirnova, 2014). Phillips’ (2011) ethnography of post-Soviet Ukraine documents the creative strategies disabled people use to claim mobile citizenship: asserting their rights by moving between the welfare state, civic activism and public life. Physical mobility and political inclusion appear, in this view, as two sides of the same problem.

Studies in Kazakhstan confirm this picture at the city level. Yespolova (2020), using grounded theory with disabled residents of Almaty, found that their voices and needs were not considered in planning and development, and that sidewalks, ramps, transport and tactile paving remained major barriers. Kaiyrly (2025) studied subsidised housing in Astana and found that ramps and lifts existed but were often poorly built or not maintained, which increased residents’ dependence on relatives. Both studies are small-scale and qualitative, and both point to the same gap between formal compliance and real usability.

Approach 2: Crowdsourcing and participatory mapping

A second body of research asks how accessibility data can be collected at scale without expensive professional audits. Wheelmap, run by the Berlin non-profit Sozialhelden, lets anyone rate places with a traffic-light scheme and stores the data in OpenStreetMap, so it works worldwide although most entries are in Germany (Mobasheri et al., 2017). Project Sidewalk moved from places to streets: volunteers and paid crowdworkers “walk” through Google Street View and label curb ramps, missing ramps, obstacles and surface problems (Saha et al., 2019). Its deployment in Washington, D.C. showed that remote crowd audits can cover a whole city, but also revealed a trade-off between scale and label quality.

Approach 3: Computer vision and AI for detecting barriers

Crowdsourced labels became the training data for the next step. Weld et al. (2019) trained deep neural networks on more than 300,000 Project Sidewalk labels to detect and validate sidewalk problems automatically; in some tasks the model matched or exceeded human labellers, but it struggled with low-resolution images and difficult lighting, and the authors had to test separately how well a model trained in one city transfers to another. Hosseini et al. (2023) built Tile2Net, an open-source tool that extracts sidewalks, crosswalks and footpaths from aerial imagery and turns them into a network that routing tools can use. It was trained on about 20,000 image tiles from four US cities.

Approach 4: Personalised pedestrian routing

The final approach asks how such data should be used. Bolten and Caspi (2021), the team behind the AccessMap routing tool in Seattle, propose “personalised pedestrian network analysis”: the same street network is weighted differently for each mobility profile. Their city-scale analysis shows that pedestrians with different needs disagree about which corridors are most important. There is no single “accessible route”, only routes that are accessible for someone.

What is known, what is contested, where research falls short

Known. Building-level accessibility does not guarantee accessible journeys; street-level barriers can be mapped by crowds and partly detected by AI; and routing must be personalised.

Contested. First, how far AI can replace human auditors: Weld et al. report near-human performance on some tasks, while the same work documents systematic errors and treats transfer between cities as an open question. Second, who should own accessibility data: volunteer platforms such as Wheelmap and OpenStreetMap, or government registries. Third, whether crowdsourcing empowers disabled people or quietly shifts the state’s monitoring work onto them as unpaid labour.

Shortfalls. The technical literature is overwhelmingly built in North America and Western Europe, on Google Street View coverage, dense volunteer mapping and English-language interfaces. Its models have not been tested on Kazakhstani streets, winter conditions or Soviet-era urban layouts. The regional social research, in turn, is qualitative and small-scale and does not use spatial data. None of the studies reviewed here evaluates audible or visual crossing signals, the barriers that matter most to blind and deaf pedestrians.

The gap this project speaks to

No study I found connects the lived experience of disabled pedestrians in Kazakhstan with an open, street-level data system that could feed routing and public policy. QalaAccess AI tests whether the tools developed in Seattle and Washington can be adapted to Astana, and whether citizen reports, supported but not replaced by AI, can make visible what official accessibility statistics currently miss.

04

The AI-Assisted Solution, and What It Would Cost

Part A — The solution: what kind of system

QalaAccess AI is a civic data system with three layers. The route planner and reporting form further down this page are a working prototype of the first and third layers; the AI layer is the proposal.

  1. Citizen reporting. Anyone can mark a barrier on a map: a missing ramp, a broken sound signal, a blocked sidewalk. Users never disclose a diagnosis.
  2. AI assistance. A multimodal AI model reads the photo attached to a report, suggests the barrier type, describes the visible evidence and states its uncertainty. A second model can scan street-level photos collected by city vehicles to propose likely barriers in areas where nobody has reported yet. AI only suggests: every suggestion goes to a human reviewer.
  3. Personalised routing and open data. Verified barriers are added to an open pedestrian network (OpenStreetMap). The planner then calculates different routes for wheelchair users, blind, low-vision, colour-blind and deaf pedestrians, and explains why each barrier matters.

Run by whom, reaching whom

I propose that the Astana akimat run the system together with organisations of persons with disabilities, which would review reports and set priorities, as the CRPD Committee recommends (UN CRPD Committee, 2024, para. 20). It reaches residents with disabilities, older people and parents with prams. The akimat receives a live inspection and repair list instead of occasional audits.

Evidence that this works elsewhere

Case 1 · United States · since 2019

Project Sidewalk + deep learning

Researchers at the University of Washington built a tool in which volunteers label sidewalk problems in street imagery (Saha et al., 2019).

The resulting dataset of more than 300,000 labels was used to train deep-learning models that detect and validate sidewalk problems, in some tasks at near-human accuracy (Weld et al., 2019). The combination of crowd labels and AI is exactly the model proposed here.

Case 2 · Global · since 2023

Be My Eyes and “Be My AI”

The Danish app Be My Eyes, used by blind and low-vision people, integrated OpenAI’s GPT-4 to describe photos users take with their phones (OpenAI, 2023). In a customer-support deployment with Microsoft the company reported that issues were solved in about 4 minutes instead of 12 (Be My Eyes, 2023).

These are company-reported figures, and independent research on similar AI assistants for blind users found hallucinations and overconfident answers that can mislead users (Chang et al., 2025). The lesson: AI image description helps, but only with human fallback.

Part B — The bill

The bill
QalaAccess AI · Astana pilot · what it would really cost
Environmental≈ 48 kWh / year · on a coal-heavy grid

For the AI layer I assume an Astana pilot with 20,000 photo analyses per year (my assumption). Google reports that a median text prompt to its Gemini model uses 0.24 Wh of electricity and 0.26 ml of water (Google, 2025). Image analysis costs more; if each analysis used ten times as much (again my assumption), the pilot would need about 48 kWh a year, a negligible amount at national scale. But about two-thirds (67%) of Kazakhstan’s electricity is generated from coal (Agora Energiewende, 2024). If the data is processed in Kazakhstan for legal reasons (see below), every kilowatt-hour is mostly coal power. Scanning a whole city’s street imagery would multiply this, and the energy and water used to train the underlying models abroad are outside the city’s control.

EconomicSoftware cheap · people & repairs expensive

Software and data are cheap: OpenStreetMap is free under an open licence, the routing engine is open-source, and this website runs on free hosting. The real costs are people and concrete: reviewers who check reports, staff who maintain the system, and above all the crews who actually fix ramps, kerbs and signals. The state budget would pay, through the akimat; Kazakhstan already spends about 1 trillion tenge a year on support for persons with disabilities (Bizmedia.kz, 2026). If the AI layer uses a foreign commercial model, part of the money flows to companies abroad and the city risks depending on one supplier.

LaborHidden, often unpaid work

Municipal inspectors would move from routine audits to targeted checks of reported barriers. The system also depends on hidden labour: disabled residents who report barriers for free, OpenStreetMap volunteers who map the network, reviewers who moderate reports, and, upstream, low-paid data annotators who labelled the images on which commercial vision models were trained. A fair design pays disability organisations for verification instead of treating disabled people as a free sensor network.

Political & governanceHigh sensitivity · data must stay in KZ

A map of where disabled people report problems, and the routes they request, is sensitive: it reveals the daily movements of a vulnerable group. Street photos capture faces and car number plates. Kazakhstan’s Law on Personal Data requires databases with personal data to be stored on Kazakh territory (Law No. 94-V, 2013), which rules out simply sending photos to a foreign AI service. The system should blur faces and plates on the user’s phone, avoid storing route requests, and run the model in the country, for example on the National AI Platform created by the new Law on Artificial Intelligence (No. 230-VIII, signed 17 November 2025, in force since January 2026). That law sets requirements for safety, transparency and accountability and requires labelling of works created with AI, so every AI suggestion in the system must be clearly marked as such. The proposal is also consistent with the UNESCO Recommendation on the Ethics of AI (2021) and the OECD AI Principles (2019, updated 2024), which call for human oversight, transparency and inclusion. Control should be shared: data stays open in OpenStreetMap, so no single agency or company owns it.

Data ethicsPhotos · locations · never health data

The system needs photos, locations and barrier types, but never health information. Reporters give consent when they submit; bystanders in photos cannot, which is why automatic blurring is essential. What could go wrong: an AI model trained on American streets misses a barrier on an icy Astana sidewalk, and the app sends someone into it; false or malicious reports close good routes; older residents without smartphones are left out. The safeguards are the same each time: AI never certifies a route as safe, unknown data is never treated as accessible, and humans verify before data affects routing.

TotalCheap in compute · expensive in people, trust and repairs
Verdict: yes, but only under conditions

The citizen reporting and personalised routing layers are cheap, low-risk and fill a real data gap, so they should be piloted in Astana now. The AI layer is worth adding only if five conditions hold:

  1. AI assists human reviewers and never certifies a route or facility as accessible.
  2. Personal data is removed on the device or processed inside Kazakhstan.
  3. Organisations of persons with disabilities co-govern the system and are paid for their work.
  4. The akimat commits a budget and deadlines to fix verified barriers. Without this, the system becomes only a more detailed map of failure.
  5. The pilot is evaluated after one year with disabled users, including whether reports led to repairs.

If these conditions cannot be met, the honest answer is no: Kazakhstan should first invest in fixing known barriers and collecting street-level data without AI.

AI transparency statement

This project was co-created by Gaziza Aushenova and Claude (Anthropic), used through Claude Code, working together the way the course asks students to work with their AI. Claude wrote all of the website’s code: the page layout, the interactive map, the route planner and the barrier-reporting form. It also built the data and routing method: it downloaded real footpath, crossing and stair data for Astana’s Nurzhol Boulevard from OpenStreetMap, turned it into a pedestrian network, and implemented Dijkstra’s algorithm with plain-language explanations of why each barrier stops a person. Gaziza chose the topic, decided what the site should and should not include, drafted the section texts and directed revisions.

What the AI Process Log for this project shows: continuous iteration, with the structure, the features and each section discussed and revised in dialogue rather than generated once. Features were proposed, tested in a browser and then kept or removed at Gaziza’s request.
